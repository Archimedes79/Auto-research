**What it does** [checked]: for each target BEV pixel i, the K nearest lidar points on the BEV plane are found, each projected into the image to bilinearly sample an image feature f_j; an MLP (3 layers) takes concat[f_j, x_j - x_i] (the 3D offset of neighbour to target) and the outputs are summed: h_i = sum_j MLP(f_j, x_j - x_i), then added to the BEV feature of the previous layer. Compared with the parametric continuous convolution the MLP outputs features directly instead of kernel weights. Four fusion layers feed four ResNet18 scales into the four residual groups of the BEV net.

**Results that matter** [checked]: KITTI test Car BEV moderate AP 85.83 at about 0.06 s per frame; TOR4D BEV vehicle AP0.7 83.89 vs 81.41 BEV-only; the gain grows with range. Ablation (KITTI val Car): removing the geometric offset input hurts significantly, KNN interpolation beats discrete fusion, performance is insensitive to the distance threshold, K = 1 to 3 got worse (best 3D moderate AP about 73.25 at K = 1, d = 10).

**Transferable technique**: the offset enters the MLP, not only the weights: so a radar version is h_i = sum_j MLP([features_j, dr, dphi, r_j, dt_j]); the "offset input matters" ablation is evidence that the geometry must be an input, not a baked-in Gaussian. K = 1 is as good as larger K there (the nearest point suffices when points are dense); for sparse radar the K needs to be re-tested.

**Cost and fit**: nearest-neighbour search on a BEV grid is cheap for radar (few points); the camera half of ContFuse (project lidar points into images) needs lidar and is not our case.

**Doubts**: lidar density is much higher than radar; no uncertainty output; the 3D offset is Cartesian, no sensor-frame polar offsets; proprietary TOR4D set.
